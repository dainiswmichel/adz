Most advertising you see online was chosen for you by a system that has been watching you. It knows the sites you visited last week, the thing you nearly bought on Tuesday, roughly where you live, what device you are holding, and how long you paused on a photograph. From that it infers what you might want, and it sells the chance to interrupt you.

Permission-based advertising inverts that. Instead of inferring what you want from surveillance, it asks you. You type what you are actually shopping for, in your own words, and that is what you are shown. Nothing is collected about you to make it work, because nothing needs to be.

The difference in practice

Suppose you are looking for a two-seater couch in a light fabric, and specifically not leather. Under surveillance advertising, the system has to guess that from your behaviour. It might notice you visited a furniture site, and for the next three weeks it will show you couches — including leather ones, including three-seaters, including couches you already bought. It cannot know you ruled leather out, because you never told anyone.

Under permission-based advertising, you write "couch: light fabric, two-seater, not leather". That is the whole mechanism. The words are yours. They are specific in a way inference never is. And when you stop looking for a couch, you delete them and the advertising stops.

Why this is better for the person being advertised to

  • It is accurate. A stated intention beats an inferred one. You know what you want; a model is guessing.
  • It ends. Surveillance advertising keeps chasing a purchase you already made. A stated intention can be withdrawn the moment it stops being true.
  • It costs you nothing. No profile is built. No identifier follows you between sites. There is nothing to leak, sell, or subpoena.
  • It is honest. You can see exactly why you were shown something, because you wrote the reason.

Why this is better for the site owner

A publisher running surveillance advertising is in an awkward position. They are paid to let third parties observe their readers, and they have very little say in what those third parties do with what they learn. The reader's irritation lands on the publisher, not on the network.

Permission-based advertising gives the publisher something to offer that is genuinely theirs: a reader who has said what they want. That is a better audience to sell to, and it is one the publisher can describe honestly to their readers without embarrassment.

What it does not solve

Permission-based advertising only works when someone chooses to use it. A reader who writes nothing sees nothing, and a site that depends entirely on advertising revenue cannot immediately replace that income with a system most of its readers have not opted into yet.

That is why adz.world is built to run alongside the advertising a site already carries, rather than instead of it. A site keeps its existing networks and adds permission-based slots into the same rotation. The proportion can shift as readers take it up. Nobody has to bet the business on a principle.

The short version

Surveillance advertising asks "what can we work out about this person?" Permission-based advertising asks the person. The second question is cheaper to answer, more accurate, and does not require anyone to be followed.
